FreeRTOS & IMU integration

Ground-vehicle localization · Sensor acquisition on an MCU · UART to Linux

My contribution

My partner and I integrated IMU acquisition on a microcontroller running FreeRTOS and forwarded measurements over UART to a Linux-based ground robot for localization alongside LiDAR. We corrected the IMU’s axis directions and accounted for its mounting orientation so the measurements matched the vehicle’s motion before entering the estimation workflow.

What the sensor supplies

The gyroscope measures angular rate, while the accelerometer measures specific force, which includes the effect of gravity. These measurements support motion estimation; the IMU does not directly report the robot’s position.

What the MCU and FreeRTOS do

The MCU provides the hardware interface to the IMU. FreeRTOS schedules sensor acquisition and measurement handling, and the embedded firmware prepares the data for transmission. UART carries the samples to the Linux computer, where the larger localization workload runs.

The RTOS design principle is to keep sensor-event handling short and perform the data reads and preparation in task context. Signaling wakes a task; buffered records carry the measurements. This keeps acquisition work separate from the more expensive estimation work on Linux.

Why measurement time matters

IMU and LiDAR data arrive at different rates and can experience different delays. The estimator needs the time a measurement represents, rather than simply the time its UART message arrived. Consistent units, axes, and measurement timing make the handoff useful for localization.

Calibration & coordinate-frame alignment

Correcting axis direction and mounting orientation

We aligned the sensor’s axis directions with the vehicle’s forward, lateral, and vertical directions, then accounted for the fixed orientation of the IMU on the robot. This gave the localization software a consistent interpretation of acceleration and angular rate during straight travel and turns.

From the sensor frame to the world frame

The sensor frame follows the IMU’s own axes. The vehicle frame follows the robot, and the world frame is the fixed reference used to describe its movement. Our axis and mounting corrections established the sensor-to-vehicle relationship. The estimator uses the robot’s changing orientation to express motion in the world frame and account for gravity consistently.

A mounting correction is fixed; the robot’s orientation changes as it moves. Keeping those two rotations distinct helps prevent a sideways sensor axis or reversed sign from being interpreted as motion along the wrong world direction.

How the EKF supports localization

Prediction from the IMU

The Extended Kalman Filter uses angular-rate and acceleration information to predict how orientation, velocity, and position evolve between observations. Its uncertainty grows as it propagates motion because sensor noise and bias accumulate.

Correction from LiDAR information

LiDAR processing compares scan geometry with a reference scan or map to provide motion or pose information. The estimator compares that information with its prediction and applies a correction according to the measurement uncertainty. This constrains the drift that accumulates when relying on inertial measurements alone.

Frame alignment is part of making those two sources agree: both must describe the same robot motion using compatible axes and reference frames. Correcting the IMU’s orientation at the integration boundary gives the filter physically consistent input.

Result

The project connected FreeRTOS sensor acquisition to a Linux localization workflow over UART. The axis-direction and mounting-orientation changes improved the consistency of the IMU data supplied for localization. My contribution centered on that embedded-to-Linux integration and the sensor-frame corrections.
